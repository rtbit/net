Bitcoin's fundamental features include: A fixed, mathematically-enforced supply cap Decentralized validation and consensus Trustless transaction verification Resistance to centralized monetary policy intervention The Austrian school of economics, which I mentioned before, informs much of my beliefs and the theoretical framework supporting bitcoin, emphasizes: The importance of sound money Skepticism toward centralized monetary policy The value of allowing market forces to operate without intervention The role of saving and capital accumulation in economic growth If you are curious about this school of thought, I recommend checking out the works of Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek in particular. Or Menger. A great introduction is Henry Hazzlitt's One Lesson in Economy :) Understanding Sound Money and Modern Monetary System Sound money, historically represented by commodities like gold, maintains its purchasing power over time and is not easily manipulated by central authorities and artificial influences (artificial in the sense that they are detached from the natural behavior of the free market). Modern fiat currencies originated as debt instruments - essentially promissory notes representing a claim on physical gold. However, this foundation was gradually removed, particularly with the abandonment of the gold standard, leaving currencies backed solely by government decree, debt, and essentially our trust. Today's monetary system operates through the creation of money primarily as debt through commercial banks, with central banks controlling the money supply through various policy tools. This system of monetary policy - the management of money supply and interest rates by central banks - aims to control inflation, stabilize economies, and promote growth. However, this approach inherently devalues savings through inflation and can lead to economic distortions (how much did the price of a good increase because it is demanded and how much of it is the effects of inflation?). The mainstream opinion of financial people in charge, proponents of so-call Keynesian principles, is that this is good - it promotes spending, and spending promotes growth. The logic being that money should erode to force it to move, and money erosion (inflation) also serves as a tool to facilitate wage reduction -- Most employees would not accept an upfront 10% wage cut, but inflation lets companies do that by simply not giving raises and make time do the work. While the number remains the same, the abstract value that it represents shrinks. Let's get into some of the toughest arguments against bitcoin and see how we can tackle them. These arguments come in no particular order. Tackling arguments against BTC Transaction Costs and Usability Bitcoin is unsuitable for day-to-day transactions due to high fees and slow processing times. At scale, it offers no advantages over fiat currency systems. Credit card companies charge merchants approximately 1-3% per transaction. While these costs are hidden from consumers through pricing, Bitcoin's Lightning Network (which is a layer 2 network built on top of BTC) already offers lower fees with complete transparency. The key difference is visibility - bitcoin shows fees upfront rather than hiding them in prices. This is a common theme. Just like the wages I mentioned above, all cuts and fees must be visible here upfront. Markets naturally encourage L2 nodes to compete for the lowest fees, and solutions like the Muun wallet and others demonstrate how users can participate without directly opening and funding channels. For on-chain transactions, users can choose their own fees based on urgency. When immediate processing isn't necessary, fees can be set much lower. Bitcoin's ability to function with minimal bandwidth makes it viable even in regions with poor internet infrastructure, capable of operating via SMS, satellite, or even radio. If you are extreme enough, you can do BTC on pen and paper. It will just take a while. However, it is still math. Bitcoin is extremely resilient, and can easily find its way into locations where a bank simply cannot. Fundamental Value Unlike most assets, cryptocurrency has no fundamental value. While bitcoin comes closest to a reasonable financial instrument, its real-world value remains more gimmick than market driver. It operates primarily as a greater fool scheme, albeit with high liquidity. The concept of "intrinsic value" is fundamentally flawed. There is no physical property called value that you can assign to any piece of matter. It is subjective. All value ultimately derives from human perception of utility, scarcity, and potential future use. Bitcoin has fundamental value through its properties as a trustless medium of exchange and store of value. Its value stems from mathematical scarcity, decentralization, and network consensus rather than government decree or trust in institutions. In general terms, and to invoke Menger, value flows backward: consumer (YOUR) valuation of final goods determines the value of production goods, not the other way around. The subjective aspect helps explain why different cultures can value things differently, and why values change over time - there's no objective "correct" value. Now, this may be viewed as copium meant to discredit stocks and fiat in face of bitcoin, but it shouldn't be. I am not saying that value is meaningless, but that it has subjective origins, and that what we perceive as the objective "value" is actually a consensus or a compromise between all the subjective personal values that market participants assign to things. The valuation of bitcoin cuts out several layers of proverbial middle-men, which are present in the valuation of stocks. Stocks also often trade at a premium to their "objective" calculated value. Inflation vs Deflation Deflation is a serious macroeconomic problem. The economy needs to create value and meet population needs and wants. This requires founding companies and investing capital. Without inflation pushing people to invest, significantly less economic activity would occur. In an inflationary system, forced spending motivates suboptimal investments, since if you are not investing money, you are actively losing money. Not missing out on potential profits, the value of the money held in your bank account actively erodes, despite the number staying the same. Artificial pressure results in misallocated capital, short-term thinking, and systematic punishment of savers. The middle class particularly struggles with the pressure to invest their savings to preserve value, increasing their vulnerability to investment scams. Haven't you or your parents been pressured by the notion that you have to do something with the money you saved? You tell me "Well, I got a savings account, I am not making any rash decision". You aren't, but the provider of said savings account has to do something with the money to pay your interest. And sure, it is insured, so if it goes bust, you will not lose money. However, there will be a cascading effect on the other side which will devalue your money. While is it true that defaulting on loans does not directly create inflation, monetary policy in response to many such occurences (ie. quantitative easing) might, and often will. A mildly deflationary environment provides clearer market signals because investments are more considered. Capital flows to genuinely productive ventures, and long-term planning is incentivized. Saving becomes a rational economic strategy. Historical precedents, such as Japan's periods of economic growth during mild deflation, demonstrate this possibility. Bitcoin isn't that prone to a deflationary spiral because holders aren't passive, rising value makes more economic activity attractive, and fundamental human needs continue driving economic activity. All historical deflationary spirals were caused by local external factors, often exacerbated by central bank decisions. Bitcoin's money creation isn't debt-based, and its supply is more adaptable to market sentiment without central authority intervention. What is a deflationary spiral? A deflationary spiral is a negative economic cycle that can occur in a deflating economy: Prices begin falling (that's deflation) People expect prices to fall further They delay purchases waiting for lower prices This reduced spending causes businesses to I. Lower prices further, II. Cut production, III. Lay off workers Laid-off workers have less money to spend This further reduces demand Leading to more price cuts, layoffs, etc. The cycle theoretically continues downward in a self-reinforcing "spiral". The Great Depression is often cited as an example. Critics of bitcoin often worry about this because its fixed supply is inherently deflationary as the economy grows. However, this ignores: Mild deflation doesn't automatically trigger this spiral People can't indefinitely delay all purchases Rising asset value can encourage spending in other areas Historical deflation spirals involved other factors (bank collapses, policy mistakes) The fact that a currency has a (mind you, mathematically given, not based on arbitrary human decisions) tendency to slightly deflate does not mean it will cause a deflationary spiral. If something is predicable, it will be priced in. Everything is priced in if it can be. Transparency is a key advantage - bitcoin transactions can be verified by anyone, and multisig features (where multiple parties have to agree) enable trustless inheritance, escrow, and other financial arrangements without intermediaries. For donation transparency, anyone can verify an xpub wallet's contents and movements. An xpub (extended public key) is a bitcoin feature that lets you generate an unlimited number of public addresses for receiving bitcoin without exposing any private keys. Think of it as a master template for creating receiving addresses. By extension, it also lets you see the contents and movements of a wallet, without having to trust anybody. When you share an xpub: Others can see all transactions to addresses derived from it They can verify these addresses belong to the same wallet They cannot spend any funds They can generate new receiving addresses Crime and Illicit Usage The main non-speculative use case for cryptocurrency is financing crime and terrorism. Practical Applications and Real-World Use Besides speculation, cryptocurrency has no real use case. The main non-speculative application is financing crime and terrorism. Bitcoin finds legitimate use cases in numerous contexts, particularly in regions with unstable financial systems. In South America and Africa, where fiat currencies are unreliable, Bitcoin serves as a practical medium of exchange. Russian expatriates use it to send money home to families when SWIFT access is restricted. In places with poor banking infrastructure but basic internet access, bitcoin's minimal bandwidth requirements make it a viable solution. For any situation where traditional banking charges percentage-based fees, bitcoin becomes immediately advantageous for larger transactions since fees relate to byte size of the transaction rather than amount transferred (admittedly, more complex transactions would be more favored in the traditional banking system, but does the traditional banking system allow you to do complex transactions?). The Lightning Network makes smaller transactions equally practical, with fees typically lower than traditional payment processors. Also, do you want your currency to be something other than money? Following the UNIX philosophy, I prefer if bitcoin is good at being money and does not try anything else. In developing regions, such as mining farms in Congo, bitcoin operations have incentivized improvements in local power infrastructure. For almost any case requiring electric heating, bitcoin mining makes economic sense as it provides returns on electricity costs even when hash price is below electricity cost. The Role of Exchanges Holding crypto on exchanges is equivalent to holding crypto in a bank, but without regulations and with a problematic business model. If crypto were truly a currency, exchanges wouldn't be needed. While exchanges serve important functions in providing liquidity and price discovery, they're not fundamental to bitcoin's operation. Unlike traditional banking, bitcoin offers the option of self-custody, giving users complete control over their assets. The problems with exchanges are not inherent to Bitcoin itself but rather to centralized institutions, which exist in any financial system. Exchanges, banks, trusts, hedge funds and other financial institutions have been having similar issues since time immemorial, and therefore we cannot simply nail bitcoin on the cross for issues that are not the fault of bitcoin, but rather institutions that deal with it. Economic Theory and Value Value fundamentally comes from human needs for food, water, shelter, and general survival. Thinking it's just an arbitrary number is crypto copium to make fiat appear as meaningless as crypto. Value extends beyond basic physical needs - humans value abstract concepts and services. Bitcoin's fundamental value lies in providing a trust-minimized medium of exchange and store of value. Its mathematical scarcity, decentralization, and network consensus create value differently but no less legitimately than government decree or physical commodities. In most general terms, value is not an inherent property of goods or services, but rather emerges from human valuation and preference. It is entirely subjective and determined by individual human preferences, not intrinsic to any object or service, but comes from the importance people place on it for satisfying their needs and wants. The concept of "intrinsic value" is fundamentally flawed - even vital goods like water have no inherent value outside of human valuation. Therefore, value exists in the mind of the economic actor making choices, and the market price of something emerges from the aggregate of these subjective valuations through voluntary exchange. (I have already mentioned some of this above, but it is helpful to repeat it here) This understanding of value is particularly relevant to bitcoin criticism. Arguments about bitcoin's lack of "intrinsic value" misunderstand that no asset - whether gold, fiat currency, or Bitcoin - has intrinsic value. Gold's value as a precious metal comes from human subjective valuation of its properties (scarcity, durability, aesthetic appeal), just as bitcoin's value comes from human valuation of its properties (mathematical scarcity, censorship resistance, decentralization --- which are in a way, also scarcity, durability and aesthetic appeal). This perspective helps explain why different societies throughout history have used various items as money - from shells to salt to precious metals. Or like, bigass fuckin stones. Their monetary value wasn't intrinsic but emerged from shared subjective valuation of their useful properties as mediums of exchange and stores of value.
